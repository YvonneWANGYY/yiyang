About

Physics, computation, and the unknown.

I am a graduate student in the Department of Physics and Astronomy at Seoul National University, supported by the Global Korea Scholarship (GKS). I work with Prof. Ji-hoon Kim on computational astrophysics and machine learning for astrophysical simulations.

My current project develops a sample-efficient calibration framework in which a conditional normalizing flow learns target-conditioned parameter proposals and an active-learning loop decides which simulation or model evaluation to run next. I am especially interested in problems where large-scale simulations, statistical inference, and machine learning meet: how we can turn complex numerical universes into interpretable physical constraints while making better use of limited computational budgets.

A large part of what draws me to computational research is the logic of programming itself. I enjoy the way a scientific idea can be translated into precise rules, algorithms, and executable structures. Studying computer science and machine learning made this attraction even stronger: it showed me that computation is not only a tool for implementing known physics, but also a powerful language for discovering patterns, building new models, and exploring the unknown.

This is why I am particularly excited by the growing connection between astrophysics and modern computer science. I hope to develop computational and machine-learning methods that can help us probe more of the universe — especially regimes where simulations are high-dimensional, observations are incomplete, and physical intuition alone is not enough.

Before SNU, I studied Physics at Shenzhen University with a minor in Computer Science, and spent an exchange year in Physics at the University of Seoul. My earlier computational work included condensed matter physics, nano-optics, and scientific programming, experiences that gradually shaped my interest in computation as a common language across different areas of physics.

M.S. research · Seoul National University

Sample-efficient machine-driven calibration for astrophysical simulations

I am developing a normalizing-flow-guided sequential active-calibration framework that separates case-specific target construction, model execution, and metric extraction from a reusable proposal-and-acquisition engine. Latin-hypercube samples provide the initial design; a conditional rational-quadratic spline flow learns target-conditioned parameter proposals; and a finite candidate pool is ranked before the next expensive evaluation.

The same outer workflow is tested across three different settings: Sedov–Taylor blast-wave profiles, Rotating Cylinder angular-momentum conservation, and local SAGE galaxy-formation calibration against multiple observational summary statistics. The goal is not to treat the learned density as an uncorrected Bayesian posterior, but to improve the best realized calibration result under a fixed evaluation budget.

In the current eight-observable local SAGE experiment, the shared best initial design reaches R² = 0.4359, while joint learned-proposal selection reaches R² = 0.7060 within 10 active evaluations. In the Sedov case, I also study cost-aware acquisition using adaptive-mesh refinement complexity, so candidate selection can account for computational cost as well as physical profile agreement.
